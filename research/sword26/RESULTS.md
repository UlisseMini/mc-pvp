# How strong is the sword bot?

The original playable checkpoint learned when to swing and when to jump. In Minecraft Java 26.3 it beat plain W-tap in 13 of 16 duels. At 100 ms added round-trip latency the result depended on which spawn seat it played. An independent bot trained with the same outcome-based approach beat it seven times in eight by playing a very different game. Both are playable in the launcher. Established combos, deliberate hit selection and performance against strong human players remain unproven.

The [from-scratch fighter and its learned jump reset](SELF-PLAY.md) are playable in option 5. The [league-trained fighter](TRAINED-FIGHTER.md) is playable in option 4. Its [fast simulator was checked against Java 26.3](SIMULATOR.md) on successive independent panels, including 96 fresh fights on the final version. The results below describe the original checkpoint.

## What the learner saw and earned

Training started from random parameters. Every policy could move forward, back and sideways, sprint, jump, swing and steer relative to the opponent. A shared aim helper tracked the opponent vertically and turned up to 45 degrees per input tick. The learner observed its own state and the opponent as displayed by its client. The only payoffs were fight outcome and health difference. The transcript, named techniques, W-tap controls and evaluation opponents were outside training and checkpoint selection.

The recorded training stages scored 3,087,680 simulated duels before the final cross-play tournament. This training run, including selection, took about two minutes and 25 seconds on this Mac with the game assets and Java already installed. A second full run with the registered seeds reproduced the playing weights byte for byte. That count excludes the extra play used to generate mid-duel starts. The tournament scored each contender in 1,152 duels against learned opponents. The winning checkpoint uses six small decision trees to issue the controls. It beat the recurrent candidates under the registered selection rule. [Training protocol](protocol.json) · [Frozen checkpoint record](evidence/frozen/registration.json)

Each pair shared a starting angle and spacing. The server and client tick phases proceeded independently in each real bout. One health point is half a heart.

## What it learned from the fights

- **Swing timing.** In the 16 duels against W-tap the trained bot issued 154 attack inputs. The server accepted 153 damaging hits, all charged. Its mean input rate over the panel was 1.29 attacks per second, compared with 1.10 for W-tap under the same 20-per-second ceiling. Its learned attack tree usually waits until charge reaches about 95% and a ray through the displayed target could connect.
- **Jump timing.** The server recorded 31 falling criticals and 82 sprint-boosted hits for the trained bot. W-tap recorded zero falling criticals and 167 sprint-boosted hits. The trained bot held jump for 1,544 of 2,380 input ticks. On the model scorecard, forcing its jump key down gave 56/64 wins and releasing it throughout gave 53/64. The original earned 63/64 and W-tap earned 32/64. These substitutions test the effect in the model. [Held-jump scores](evidence/frozen/jump-held.csv) · [Released-jump scores](evidence/frozen/jump-released.csv)
- **Movement still missing.** It held W on every one of those 2,380 ticks. It never pressed back or strafe in that panel. The policy consequently offers no demonstration of S-taps or strafing to maintain spacing. Its attack inputs and replays also give insufficient evidence of deliberate hit selection, flyouts or predicting a specific opponent's next hit.

## A second bot learned to give ground

The independent tree bot in launcher option 2 learned a different movement rule from its own fights. Its forward/backward tree switches at roughly **82% sword charge**. Across all 16 recorded bouts as the opponent, it moved back on every one of 805 ticks below half charge, and moved forward on every one of 1,243 ticks at full charge. It backed up on 1,204 ticks overall, including 1,100 moving diagonally backward. The referee counted 85 critical hits. The implementation of the trainer has no instruction to back up after an attack or to re-engage at that threshold.

There were 20 incoming fresh hits where the last sampled input before server contact was backward. Three were followed by two consecutive samples with both clients out of reach before another hit, within half a second. One of the three also had jump held at impact. That records individual examples consistent with the flyout in the video. It leaves open whether the bot anticipated the incoming hit. The much more frequent pattern is disengaging during its own cooldown. These labels are offline measurements. [Opponent key and contact counts by fight](evidence/frozen/independent-native-audit.json)

I then tested the rival on fresh, paired real openings. One version forced its forward key on throughout. All its other learned control trees and the aim helper stayed the same. Across eight real duels the original won **7/8**, forward-held won **4/8** and plain W-tap won **2/8**. From seat A both learned variants won 4/4. From seat B the original won 3/4 and forward-held won 0/4. The movement advantage was concentrated in that seat. The sample demonstrates a benefit on these starts. Neither version established a three-hit unanswered streak after the defender began clicking.

## After a combo is established

The landmark was three consecutive unanswered fresh hits after the defender first pressed attack. Five and ten were scored separately. Any accepted return, including a damage upgrade, ended a streak. A second score also ended a streak at a gap of more than 22 ticks between fresh hits. Time remaining at a round end was censored.

Neither arm reached even the three-hit landmark in the real W-tap comparison. The W-tap-versus-W-tap model offered zero common three-hit starts for a fork. In the model against the independent population opponent, 12 established three-hit states could be replayed with identical positions, pending effects, input histories and clock. From those exact states the frozen bot completed **two** more fresh-hit cycles in total and W-tap completed **six**. All 12 continuations per arm ended on a return. Remaining fresh hits ranged from 0–1 and 0–2 respectively, with exposures of 2–15 and 2–33 ticks. Inactivity scoring gave the same result. The one five-hit state ended on a return after five ticks for the bot and seven for W-tap, both with zero more cycles. There was no ten-hit fork. Against the independent gradient opponent there were two three-hit starts. Both arms had zero additional cycles, one return after eight ticks and one round-end censor after one tick. The raw files include every start and suffix. [W-tap forks](evidence/frozen/maintenance.csv) · [Population forks](evidence/frozen/maintenance-population.csv) · [Gradient forks](evidence/frozen/maintenance-gradient.csv)

In the independent native matches the only three-hit entry for the trained bot was against the tree opponent, with a return on the same tick. W-tap reached three twice against the recurrent opponent and three times against the tree. Its two longest remaining runs against the tree each added one fresh hit. Neither arm reached five in these real matches. The exact entry tick, exposure, right-censoring and both stop rules are in the [independent audit](evidence/frozen/independent-native-audit.json).

A separate spacing score sampled the next fresh damage window after every own fresh hit against W-tap, regardless of when the player chose to attack again. The original recordings identify this nominal window by adding ten server ticks to the accepted fresh hit. The trained bot had geometrical reach on 47/139 scorable windows, including nine with reach only in its own client view. W-tap had reach on 9/160, including two with own-view reach only. All 139 and 160 windows respectively passed without a fresh hit on that exact tick. Fourteen and seven windows respectively were censored at round end. This measures displayed eye-to-hitbox distance and exact-tick timing. A missed tick can still be followed by a winning charged hit. An instrumentation check found that the original referee logged entity invulnerability, a different field from the player's damage cooldown. The published rescoring uses the server's post-contact hurt animation on those original recordings. Across all 2,106 damaging events in the four panels it reset to ten on every hit, with at least ten ticks before the same defender took another hit. This separates fresh hits from upgrades on these recordings. The raw archives preserve the original columns. Updated recordings capture the actual damage cooldown before attacks and once per server tick.

Eight more real duels checked the corrected instrumentation at zero and 100 ms. The timer rose to 20 on fresh damage and crossed the eligibility threshold of 10 exactly ten ticks later in all 154 observable windows across both seats. The remaining eight windows were cut off by the end of a round. Both the timer and the post-hit animation classified all 162 accepted hits as fresh. The original bot and its W-tap control each won 3/4 in this small calibration panel. ## Where the training model gives bad advice

In eight development W-tap mirror bouts, median one-step movement error was about 0.00001 blocks. Once a full duel was replayed with the recorded controls, the two paths diverged by more than a quarter block around the opening hit. Median whole-replay errors by bout ranged from 3.4 to 17.9 blocks. One specific discrepancy is sprint after contact. In zero-latency development mirrors the model's local movement step predicted continued sprint incorrectly on 74 of 80 charged forward attack inputs. At 100 ms it matched the next observed sprint flag on all 85 such inputs. In the final native control arm W-tap successfully delivered sprint knockback on every damaging hit. A diagnostic one-tick increase in view and impact delay alone did not resolve the model's optimistic high-latency result. These measurements identify a contact and timing problem. They do not establish a single cause for all three losses. [Development replay errors](evidence/response/parity-development.csv)

## Can another policy learn to beat it?

After freezing the first bot, two specialist policies were trained to beat it using duel outcomes. A further learner trained against a field that included those specialists. This was an exploratory test of how much the simulator's answer survived real contact. On 32 paired model duels per opponent, the original won 2 against specialist A and 3 against B. W-tap won 8 and 1. The further learner won 32 and 28 against the same opponents. Against W-tap itself, the original won 32, the further learner won 26 and the W-tap mirror won 16.

In the fresh real-client comparison against specialist A, the original won 3/4 at each latency. The further learner won 4/4 at each. W-tap won 2/4 at zero added latency and 1/4 at 100 ms. Specialist A's near-sweep over the original in the model did not transfer. The further learner remains an experimental checkpoint because this real comparison covered one targeted opponent. ## Transition to the fast simulator

A checkpoint selected in a 40-bout native development tournament subsequently won 16/32 ordinary TCP validation bouts. The matched W-tap control won 8/32. Against the original, retreating, previously unseen and W-tap opponents respectively, the trained policy won 4/8, 5/8, 3/8 and 4/8. Its control won 3/8, 0/8, 1/8 and 4/8. That policy missed the registered whole-fight promotion rule. The native files give HP and opening-by-opening spread separately for zero and 100 ms.
